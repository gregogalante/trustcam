From TrustCam to VCAP

TrustCam was an experiment. It worked well enough to become a real project, and that project is VCAP.

Why it moved on

TrustCam started with one question: can an ordinary phone take a photo or a video and prove, later, that nobody touched it? I built the quickest thing that could answer it: one app, one web page to check files, one phone to test on.

The answer was mostly yes. The phone can seal a file the moment it is taken, and the seal can be checked by anyone in a browser. Even when a social network strips the seal away, a mark hidden in the image can often point back to the original.

But a quick experiment is not something people should rely on. The rules lived only in my code, nobody else could check them, and building fast meant cutting corners. VCAP is the same idea done properly: the rules are written down for anyone to read, they are tested, and they work across several apps and devices, not just mine.

This site stays online as it was, the record of the experiment, mistakes included.

What TrustCam got wrong

Looking back at the prototype with fresh eyes, I found things that did not hold up. Each one is fixed in VCAP.

It said "verified" too easily

The checker gave the same green "verified" to a file signed by a real phone's secure chip and to one signed by a key anyone could make on a laptop. The difference was there, but in small print. VCAP's answer is never stronger than what was actually proven, and it says what is missing.

Part of the proof could be rewritten

The seal protected the picture, but not the details next to it: who took it, with which phone, and when. Someone could change those and the file would still look genuine. In VCAP the seal covers everything the answer relies on.

A video could be shuffled

Videos were sealed piece by piece, but nothing tied the pieces together or kept them in order. Someone could drop, reorder or mix in pieces of another recording and each one would still check out. In VCAP the pieces are linked, counted and kept in order, so a shuffled video is caught.

Some hidden data in photos was not checked

To make room for other provenance labels, the checker skipped certain hidden sections inside a photo. It skipped too many, so something could be slipped in without breaking the seal. VCAP skips only the one section it has to, and recognises it precisely.

The hidden mark was treated like proof

The mark hidden in the pixels is useful for finding the original, but anyone can add one to any image. TrustCam still presented a match almost as if it were a verification. VCAP treats it only as a clue, never as proof, and on video it says how much of the clip actually carries the mark.

It promised more than it measured

Some pages turned a few tests into guarantees, gave different numbers for the same test, called the project "zero infrastructure" while it relied on outside services, or claimed compliance with a standard without support. VCAP states what was measured, what was not, and what each service it uses can see.

Small things a careful reviewer would catch

The date on a file was trusted even when it came only from the phone's clock; some security checks on the phone's certificates were missing; a damaged video file could crash the checker. All of these are handled in VCAP.

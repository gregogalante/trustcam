From TrustCam to VCAP

TrustCam asked whether capture-time provenance could work on a commodity phone. The answer was a qualified yes, and the project now continues as VCAP. This page explains the move and lists the problems found in this prototype that VCAP fixes.

VCAP Specification Public verifier github.com/VCAP-org

What changed

TrustCam was a feasibility study: one app, one verifier, one device model, and a proof format defined by whatever the code happened to do. VCAP keeps the two-layer idea, a hardware-backed signature plus an invisible watermark, and rebuilds everything around it as a specification first.

  • A written, versioned format. The proof format, JSON Schema, watermark layouts and threat model are public under the MIT licence. An independent verifier can be written from the specification alone.
  • Conformance vectors. Every rule comes with test files and the outcome a verifier must return, so two implementations can be checked against each other rather than trusted.
  • Several implementations. SDKs for Android, iOS, React Native and the web, and a public verifier that runs entirely in the browser.
  • Graded answers. Instead of one "verified" headline, the verifier returns an outcome from the specification and a colour that never exceeds what was actually proven: the hardware level, the trusted time, the key's status.
  • Infrastructure where it is needed, and stated. TrustCam described itself as having "zero infrastructure" while relying on a timestamp authority, Google's revocation list and a static file of originals. VCAP names its registry, its public transparency log and its timestamp providers, and states what the verifier can prove without them.

VCAP does not read TrustCam proofs: the formats are incompatible by design, so no constraint from the prototype carries over. This site stays online unchanged as the record of the experiment, including its mistakes.

Problems in TrustCam that VCAP fixes

A review of this prototype's verifier and documents found the issues below. They are listed here because the prototype's pages still describe the design as it was.

Verifier

Issue in TrustCamIn VCAP
The VERIFIED headline and the CLI's exit code depended on the signature alone. A key generated in software, with a chain not rooted at Google or no chain at all, received the same headline; the attestation result was a secondary line. The answer's colour is capped by the hardware level the attestation chain actually proves. A key that proves no hardware never reaches green.
The signature covered only the file hash. Name, device model, security level, capture time, capture id and the timestamp token sat in unsigned JSON next to it and could be rewritten without breaking the seal. A signed core covers every field the verdict relies on. The timestamp is taken over that core, not only over the media hash.
Each per-GOP video signature carried its own public key, which was never compared with the attested key. GOP indices were not checked, so groups could be reordered, removed or taken from another recording. Segments are chained to each other and their count is in the signed core. Every group of frames must be accounted for, in order, each at most once; segment timing is signed too.
For JPEG, any APP11 segment whose first bytes contained "JP", "jumb" or "c2pa" was left out of the hash, so such a segment could be added or altered without breaking the seal. Two hash inputs were accepted, the stripped bytes and the full bytes. Only the one segment that is a C2PA manifest store is excluded, identified by its structure.
The signature was ECDSA over SHA-256 of a SHA-256 hash, described ambiguously as "SHA256withECDSA over H". Signatures were in DER form. One hash, ES256 over the canonical (JCS) core. A fixed-length r‖s encoding with low-s required from writers.
A watermark-only match exited with the same code as a verified file, under "ORIGIN TRACED — verified original ON FILE", although the verifier never re-checked that original. A watermark alone is origin traced at most and never authentic. On video the verifier must report how many sampled frames carry the id, so one marked frame spliced into foreign footage cannot pass as a marked recording.
The attestation challenge was never read. Intermediate certificates were not checked to be CAs, the key's origin was not checked, and the security level did not affect the verdict. Certificate windows were checked against the current time rather than the capture time. Six required attestation rules, among them CA constraints, origin = GENERATED, leaf key equal to the signing key, locked bootloader with verified boot, and validity at the proven instant. The proven level is the weaker of the two the chain reports.
The timestamp check did not verify the timestamping key usage or the signer's validity at the time it signed. There was a single provider. Six required timestamp checks, including key usage and validity at the signed time. Two providers. No green without a trusted time source.
The MP4 parser read box tables without bounds checks, so a malformed file could crash the verifier. A fixed 16-byte footer with version and CRC-32, structural checks before anything else, and dedicated tests on hostile inputs.

Claims and documentation

Issue in TrustCamIn VCAP
"Never silently wrong (0 miscorrections in 31,000 trials)" stated an empirical result as a guarantee. A BCH decoder with radius 18 over 252 bits accepts a random word with a probability of roughly 2−34. The guaranteed radius is stated in bits. The point where a real channel stops recovering is marked as not measured, and false positives have their own measurement.
The 24-bit video mark id was described three ways: as a random device UUID, as device id plus counter, and as a random id. Collisions were not discussed. The mark id is derived from the 128-bit capture id. Collision probabilities are tabulated, and the mark is defined as a lookup hint only.
The photo payload was described as 256 bits while its parts added up to 252. The layout is given exactly: 128 data bits, 124 parity bits and 4 padding bits.
"Which device recorded it" referred to an id the app chose itself, with no registry behind it. The device key id is derived from the public key and recorded in a public transparency log. The format states that it proves a key, not who held the phone.
Watermark recovery was said to fail "below roughly half of the original area", but a 1:1 crop keeping 75% of the frame failed. The same channels were reported with different agreement figures on different pages. Robustness is reported per channel as bit errors and agreement under a pinned toolchain. Channels are named as simulations of a platform, not as the platform itself.
Video embedding was described as "about 2× short of real-time", while 1 s per keyframe against 33 ms per frame at 30 fps is roughly 30× short. Latency is reported only where it was measured, and on-phone cost is marked as not yet measured.
The C2PA flow was said to match "Conformance Level 2" while it was signed with development certificates. No conformance level is claimed. VCAP does not sign C2PA claims yet and says so; its proof can travel inside a C2PA manifest.
Some pages said "timestamped by a third-party authority" without noting that offline captures carry only the device's clock. The device clock is a declaration and never yields green. The proven time is the timestamp, then a blockchain anchor, then the device clock, in that order.
A losslessly trimmed clip was said to keep "segment-level integrity", but the group at the cut failed exactly like a tampered one, and a reader could not tell a cut from an edit. Frames before the first keyframe belong to no segment. A trimmed clip is verified clip (amber), distinct from tampered (red), and a clip whose frames cannot be tied to the signed ones says so.

The full rules are in the VCAP specification. The TrustCam pages and code are kept as they were.

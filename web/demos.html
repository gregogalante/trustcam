Photo — through WhatsApp and Instagram

Captured with the app on a Motorola moto g75 (full 4:3, sealed with timestamp and Content Credentials), shared on WhatsApp and posted on Instagram, then saved back. Both re-encoded the image and stripped everything attached — the mark in the pixels brought the original back.

How much survived

Both copies threw away most of the original pixels — and the capture id still came back exactly. The numbers:

WhatsApp copy
halved each side, file 8× smaller — id recovered exactly; error correction repaired 16 of the 18 bits it can fix (the verifier's full-resolution retry did the work)
Instagram post
downscaled 2.1× and recompressed — id recovered exactly, at the correction limit: all 18 repairable bits used
Verdict
both copies: Origin traced — verified original on file, shown side by side

Instagram hands photos back as HEIC (original HEIC — the copy above is a JPEG conversion, the mark decodes in both). Browsers without a HEIC decoder can't scan those pixels; the verifier now says so explicitly — convert to JPEG or use the CLI.

Photo — where the limit honestly is

Two real failure cases, published on purpose: the verifier reports No proof found rather than guessing.

Why the mark is lost — and the countermove

Telegram photo mode
the harshest pipeline we measured: down to 960×1280 (a third of the original side) plus heavy recompression — the surviving signal drops below what the error correction can repair. Countermove: send it as a file — Telegram then delivers the exact bytes, and the copy verifies green with the full cryptographic seal, timestamp included.
Instagram square
the 1:1 crop throws away 25% of the frame — beyond the error-correction budget even after the verifier restores the original framing. Posts that keep the frame (or the standard 4:5 crop) verify fine.
Why we show this
a verifier that can't say "nothing found" honestly can't be trusted when it says "found". These two files are the proof that ours does.

Video — through WhatsApp, Telegram, Instagram and YouTube

Same capture, four platforms, four different compressions — YouTube even re-encoded it to a different codec (AV1). The mark survived all of them; every copy resolves back to the verified original.

How much survived

Four platforms, four different compressions — one of them a codec the original never touched — and the mark outlived them all. The numbers:

WhatsApp
75% of the pixels gone, file 8× smaller — mark signal 80%, capture id recovered
Telegram
re-encoded at 720p — mark signal 85% (video survives Telegram; only its photo pipeline is harsher, see above)
Instagram
re-encoded at 720p — mark signal 87%
YouTube Short
re-encoded to AV1 — mark signal 82% · live at youtube.com/shorts/gI_J4PL-WWQ
Verdict
all four: Origin traced — verified original on file, shown side by side

Every platform re-encode also destroys the per-segment bitstream signatures — by design: signatures prove integrity and die on any change, the watermark is the layer built to survive. Mark signal measures how reliably the mark was decoded — origin, never authenticity.

Video — the edited copy

The same video posted on Instagram with a caption and a sticker added. The mark survives the edit — so the verifier still finds the original, puts the two side by side, and the additions have nowhere to hide. This is exactly what "origin, not authenticity" means: we prove where it comes from, the comparison shows what changed.

The numbers

What the verifier says

What your eyes say
play both: the caption and the sticker are additions the original never had

Video — the trimmed clip

The original cut losslessly (ffmpeg -ss 2 -t 4 -c copy — a stream copy, no re-encode), which strips the whole-file seal. But every ~1s segment was signed individually at capture: the surviving segments still verify cryptographically, and the mark still finds the original.

What survives a cut

Segments
4 of 5 GOPs carry valid hardware signatures — the boundary segment is cut mid-GOP and honestly fails; the seal and manifest are gone with the trim
Mark
signal 97% — the id still resolves to the verified original
Why it matters
a newsroom can cut a clip for length and still prove, segment by segment, that what remains is untouched — nothing else on a consumer phone does this
